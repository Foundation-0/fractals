Witness Map

Every object here is asked the same question: can it be in two places at once, a set gap apart? The answer comes down to a race between two clocks.

Exploration, not peer reviewed, no new result claimed. By Aska Digital with Claude (Anthropic) for Foundation0. Model, checks and open questions: Foundation-0/fractals.

Wave clock

How long the object's wave needs to spread across the gap. Heavier things spread slower, so their clock runs long.

Witness clock

How long until something in its surroundings (a photon, a gas atom, its own heat glow) carries off a record of which place it's in.

Witness number W = wave clock ÷ witness clock
W below 1: the wave wins, it can act quantum W above 1: witnesses win, it acts classical within 10× of the line nothing witnesses it

What the map shows

    The crowd: where the map stops

    not a witness number

    Every row above asks about one particle. Wave dark matter in a galaxy is never one particle. Its wave breaks into lumps, and each lump is shared by so many particles that together they act as one ordinary wave, the way countless photons make one radio wave. The crowd keeps its own clocks, and they are measured against the age of the universe, not against a gap.

    And the crowd is watched. One particle's pull is far too weak to leave a record, but a lump is a vast number of particles pulling together, and every star it passes is nudged. A nudge of that size is enormous by quantum standards, so the star now holds a record of where the lump was. The wave survives anyway: recording the shape of an ordinary wave does not erase it, any more than measuring a sea wave flattens the sea.

    Order of magnitude, like the map. Lump lifetime ħ/(mσ²), lump mass π3/2ρ(ħ/mσ)³ and stirring time from Bar-Or, Fouvry & Tremaine 2019; the core is the ground state of the Schrödinger–Poisson equations, checked against Schive et al. 2014 and 2020; breathing from Veltmaat et al. 2018.     How it's computed

    Everything runs live in this page from first principles. Treat the numbers as order-of-magnitude: the method is good to within a factor of a few, and the map spans more than 150 powers of ten.

    wave clock = m·Δx² / ħ   ·   witness rate Γ = Σ (hits per second) × (information per hit)   ·   W = Γ × wave clock
    • Hits per second. Photons from the surroundings (a full thermal spectrum at each source's temperature), gas atoms, solar wind, and the object's own heat glow. Cross-sections are geometric for objects bigger than the wavelength and fall off as (size/wavelength)⁴ for smaller ones. Electrons use the standard electron–photon value. Molecules glow through one fitted number, set so a heated fullerene loses its interference at the measured temperature.
    • Information per hit. A hit with wavelength λ can only tell the two places apart if the gap is comparable to λ. The fraction is 1 − exp(−(2πΔx/λ)²/3) for a scattered photon and 1 − sin(2πΔx/λ)/(2πΔx/λ) for one the object gives off: tiny for gaps much smaller than λ, one full record for larger gaps.
    • Black hole horizon. Γ = G⁶M⁵m²Δx⁴ / (ħc¹⁰b¹⁰) for distance b, and an electric version for the electron. Valid only when the gap is much smaller than the distance.
    • The crowd. For particle mass m, density ρ and speed spread σ: lumps about πħ/(mσ) across, living ħ/(mσ²), each pulling like a body of mass π3/2ρ(ħ/mσ)³. The stirring time is how long their tugs take to change a star's speed by as much as the halo's own spread of speeds.

    Checks against published numbers

    • Light and gas scattering match the closed-form decoherence formulas to within 2%. They match 11 of the 15 entries of the Joos–Zeh table and 7 of the 8 entries of Schlosshauer's table to within a factor of 5. The tables give powers of ten only, and where they disagree with the model they also disagree with each other.
    • The horizon formula reproduces both worked examples in the Wald paper: Earth split by its own radius beside a Sun-mass black hole (paper: ~10 µs, here: ~20 µs) and an electron split by 1 m (paper: ~10⁴³ years, here: ~4×10⁴² years).
    • Heated C₇₀, the molecule of the 2004 Vienna heating experiment, loses half its fringe visibility here at about 2,475 K, against about 2,500 K measured. The glow is fitted to that point, and the fit gets the light itself wrong: too little of it is visible.
    • The crowd's lump mass and stirring time reproduce the worked numbers of Bar-Or, Fouvry and Tremaine (2019) to 2%. The core, solved here from first principles, matches the density and mass formulas of Schive, Chiueh and Broadhurst (2014) to about 2% and their core wave period (2020) to 1%.
    • All of these run as automated checks (npm test). Mistakes and disagreements found along the way are in CORRECTIONS.md.

    What it leaves out

    • Objects are treated as sitting still. Fast motion, internal structure and spin are ignored.
    • The wave clock assumes speeds far below light speed and is unreliable for gaps below ħ/(mc), the reduced Compton wavelength. The particle horizon example also assumes a source confined to a small region, which is not justified at those scales.
    • W is per particle. Wave dark matter's galaxy-wide wave is a crowd effect, enormous numbers of particles sharing one wave. The crowd panel above gives its clocks; it has no witness number.
    • Gravity's only witness here is a black hole horizon. Gravitational nudges from passing stars are treated as zero.
    • Light an object absorbs from its surroundings is not counted. Molecules have no electronic gap, and their glow below 2,000 K is unchecked.
